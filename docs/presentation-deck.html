The premise
The app answers one question a day — what should I actually do with my child today?

Four things. One for each area of development. Sized for the age they are this week. If you do nothing else in the app, you've had a good day.

Why this, and not the alternatives

Every parenting app
hands you more work.

The content library

BabyCenter, What to Expect. Thousands of articles you never read. The work is search — you have to already know what your problem is called.

The tracker

Feeds, naps, nappies. Turns your baby into a spreadsheet, and the work is data entry. Miss two days and it becomes a guilt machine.

The third option

Tell the parent four things to do today, sized to their child's exact age, and then get out of the way. No searching. No logging debt.

Four activities. Every day.
One per domain.

Why four, and why those four. A parent doing plenty of talking and no crawling practice has a gap they can't see. One activity per domain makes the balance structural — you can't accidentally over-index on the thing you're already good at.

Four decisions behind one screen

The plan is a row,
not a render.

  • Generated once, then fixed. The four activities are written to daily_plans the first time you open the app that day. They don't reshuffle while you're looking at them.
  • "Today" is family-local. Rollover uses the timezone captured at signup, not the server's. UTC would roll an Indian family over at 5:30am — mid-morning.
  • Same on every device. Because it's persisted, you and your co-parent open the app to the identical day.
  • Swap keeps the balance. Don't like one? You get a different activity in the same domain. You can never end up with two motor tasks and no talking.

Vaccinations — 54 entries, three tiers

Essential (21) — the government UIP schedule, free at any public facility. Recommended (26) — what the IAP adds on top. Situational (7) — outbreak or geography dependent.

Where the two schedules disagree, both entries are kept with a note rather than silently picking one. Rows say "Due" — never "overdue". The app can't know what happened at the clinic.

Live demo — You
The part I'm proudest of
Every parenting app is about the child. The parent is treated as a logistics layer.

But in the first year the parent is also recovering — often badly, usually alone, and nobody is asking. So there's a whole tab for them: their own daily check-in, nutrition that accounts for feeding and recovery, and long-form care content. Seven care areas, 35 topics.

Watch the colour. The child side is warm cream; the parent side is a cooler eucalyptus at the same lightness. A parent opening it at 3am should feel the room change temperature — not the lights go out.

A product decision worth defending

Same two questions.
Completely different
relationship.

Two questions shape the parent side: how you're feeding, and how your baby arrived. They used to sit in onboarding, step five of six.

They are the two most loaded questions in postpartum life. A caesarean can carry real grief. Formula feeding carries stigma most parents have already been judged for. And we were asking both before the parent had received a single thing from us — at the last gate before the app even existed.

So I moved them. They're now asked the first time you deliberately step into your own space. The parent has already chosen to be there, so the question has an obvious reason — and anyone who never opens that tab is never asked at all. Both optional. "Rather not say" is a real answer. The button is never disabled.

Live demo — Ask

The Copilot is real now.

A conversational layer that knows which child you're asking about and how old they are. It was a scaffold six weeks ago — it answers for real today.

The key can't live in the app

Expo ships every EXPO_PUBLIC_* variable inside the JS bundle — readable by anyone who opens the app. So the model call goes through a Supabase Edge Function, and the API key stays a server-side secret that only that function ever sees.

Ownership is proved by the database

The function forwards the caller's own access token and builds a client scoped to that user. Row-level security — not the function's own logic — is what proves the child belongs to them. Same rule as every other table.

One running thread per child, persisted, so it survives a reload. The system prompt narrows its subject depending on whether you opened Ask from the Child side or the You side — one product, not two.

The one deliberately shared corner

Community, backed by Postgres.

Until two weeks ago this was an in-memory array. Nothing anyone posted survived a refresh, and no two parents ever saw each other's posts. It's now five real tables.

  • Different rules from the rest of the app. Any signed-in parent may read every discussion; they may only write their own. Enforced in RLS, so a bug in my query code can't become a way to edit someone else's post.
  • Moderation exists. Report, block an author, hide a reply, and a verified-expert flag — built in from the start rather than retrofitted after the first incident.
  • Posts are age-relevant. Discussions carry an age range, so a parent sees what's live for parents at their stage.

Under the app

Four decisions that
shaped the schema.

  • Plans are persisted, not regenerated. daily_plans, unique per child per date. The generator only runs when the row is absent.
  • Dates are family-local. An IANA timezone on the profile, captured from the device. A plan rolls over at your midnight.
  • The activity log snapshots its content. When you complete something, the title, domain and age band are stored as columns — not a pointer. So when I edit the content library next month, your child's history doesn't silently rewrite itself.
  • Every row is locked to the family that owns it. 26 row-level security policies in the database itself, so ownership holds regardless of what the app does.

Plus: completion is offline-tolerant — tap with no signal, it looks instant and reconciles later, and the server always wins. And started_at is tracked separately from completed_at, so an abandoned activity is observable and starting something never inflates "2 of 4".

Content as source code

From 56 activities
to 1,149.

The library started at two activities per domain per age band — enough to demo, not enough to live with. One swap exhausted the pool.

1,149activities in the library
28age bands, three months each, 0–7 years
7developmental domains covered
41activities available in every single band

It's authored in a CSV, compiled into a migration by a script, and applied like any other schema change. So it's diffable, reviewable, version-controlled — and it cannot drift from its source, because the seed is generated rather than hand-maintained.

The navigation was rebuilt three times

I shipped an idea
users had to learn.

FIRST Three tabs Home, Copilot, Growth. Clean, but the parent had nowhere to exist in it.
THEN Two modes, one toggle Child Mode and Parent Mode, each with four tabs, swapped by a header control. It worked — but "which mode am I in" became something the user had to hold in their head.
NOW Five tabs, no toggle Home · Community · Ask · Child · You. The colour still shifts as a wayfinding cue, but nothing about it is a concept anyone has to learn. They just pick a tab.

The mode toggle was a clever solution to a problem I'd invented. Deleting it removed a concept from the product and cost nothing.

Bugs that taught me something

The first diagnosis
was wrong.

  • A vocabulary that shrank. The website's timeline said "1–3 words" at 10–12 months and "1–2 words" at 12–15. Also, cognitive development was promised in the copy and silently dropped by a slice(0, 3) in the generator. Content bugs, not styling bugs — and invisible unless you actually read your own product.
  • A card that scrolled inside itself. Decorative blobs positioned outside a card's edges still count toward scrollHeight. The card became scrollable despite overflow-hidden, and tabbing to the button scrolled it by exactly the blob's overshoot. It would have hit every keyboard user.
  • I blamed the wrong thing first. I restructured a component around a theory about CSS filters. The measurements came back identical — which is what disproved it. The real cause only appeared because I kept measuring after I thought I'd found it.
Where it actually stands

What's real, and
what isn't.

Working

Onboarding, the four-activity Home, Discoveries, Vaccinations, the whole parent side, the AI Copilot, Community, and the entire data layer. Real accounts, real database, verified end to end against production.

Scaffolded

Reports, the Development Kit and the Product Guide are routed, designed and navigable, with content still to come. Courses and Workshops have structure and seeded lessons, but the video URLs are placeholders.

Needs a clinician

The vaccination schedule is transcribed accurately from the UIP and IAP sources, but it's medical content in a parenting app. It needs a paediatrician to sign it off before anyone relies on it. I'm not qualified to be the last set of eyes on that.

Not yet built

No analytics — which means I currently cannot answer "did anyone come back on day two?". No payments, no push infrastructure. Milestone coverage thins out after age five.

Known flaw

We ask how you gave birth without first establishing whether you're the birthing parent. For an adoptive parent or a father, that question is wrong on its face. The fix is small — ask the relationship first — and it's the kind of thing you only catch by reading your own product back slowly.

I audited my own product in August

Then I fixed
what I found.

Closed since the audit

Community was mock data — fabricated parents and fabricated expert replies, shipped to production. Now real Postgres with moderation.

Copilot forgot everything on reload. The tables existed; nothing wrote to them. Conversations now persist per child.

Still open, and correctly so

Zero analytics. No payments. Course videos are placeholders. And the waitlist has 11 signups — it isn't a pipeline, it's a form on a page nobody visits yet.

The strategic conclusion I reached, and still hold: don't build more features. The single question that decides whether this is a business is whether a parent opens it on day seven — and I have no instrumentation to answer that.
