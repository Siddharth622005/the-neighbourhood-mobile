Each phase only became possible because the one before it finished. You can't widen a product that has no spine, and you can't give a spine to something that doesn't exist yet.

PHASE 0111–12 JULY · WEBSITE

Prototype the idea
before building it.

The first thing I built wasn't the app. It was a page on the website — /today and /day, a "One Day, Lived" walkthrough — that showed a parent what a single day inside this product would feel like.

Why start there

A webpage takes a day. An app takes weeks. If "four things to do today" reads as homework rather than help, I wanted to find that out on the cheap page, not after building the expensive thing.

What it settled

The daily-activity loop was worth building, and the tone had to be permission-giving rather than instructional. Both of those decisions survived all the way into the shipped app.

PHASE 0222–23 JULY · WEBSITE

Writing the pitch
defined the product.

An editorial homepage, built in two days: a video hero, "Why We Exist", the developmental argument, and a milestone timeline. Values written around the TRICK framework. The AI companion named explicitly for the first time.

  • The homepage became the spec. You can't write a landing page for something you can't explain in a sentence. Forcing the copy forced the definition — and the definition is what the app was then built against.
  • Structure emerged here too. Story, Values and FAQ moved off the homepage onto their own routes. The homepage's job is the pitch; those pages are for someone who wants more before committing.
PHASE 0321–25 JULY · APP

From nothing to
a phone in four days.

Foundation and onboarding, then the navigation, then the screen the whole product is built around: four activities a day, one per developmental domain.

One question per screen

Onboarding was split so each screen asks one thing. The birthday gets its own screen — everything downstream is computed from that one date.

Auth turned off

Onboarding silently creates a real account behind the scenes, so security still works — but the parent never sees a sign-up step.

Installable from a link

Shipped as a web build you add to your home screen. No app store, no TestFlight. Testers were one link away by day five.

This phase is where the product first existed as a thing you could hold. It was also entirely fake underneath — nothing was saved anywhere.

PHASE 0426–27 JULY · APP

Giving it a spine.

Two days that turned a demo into a product: a real Postgres schema, seeded content, and every screen rewired to read and write it. I probed the live database rather than trusting my own migration files — and found that the tables I thought existed had never actually been applied.

  • Plans are persisted, not regenerated. Today's four activities are a row. They don't reshuffle while you look at them, and they're identical on your partner's phone.
  • Time is family-local. Rollover uses your timezone, not the server's. UTC would roll an Indian family over at 5:30 in the morning.
  • History is snapshotted. Completing an activity stores what it said at the time. When I edit the content library next month, your child's history doesn't silently rewrite itself.
  • Every row is locked to its family — in the database itself, so it holds regardless of what the app does.
PHASE 0530 JULY – 10 AUGUST · APP

The subject widened
from child to family.

Every parenting app treats the parent as a logistics layer — the person who administers the app to the baby. But in the first year the parent is also recovering, often badly, usually alone, and nobody is asking. So they got their own side of the product: seven care areas, 35 topics, a daily check-in, nutrition staged to how they're feeding and healing.

And one decision I'd defend anywhere. Two questions shape that side: how you're feeding, and how your baby arrived. They used to be in onboarding, step five of six — the two most loaded questions in postpartum life, asked before the parent had received a single thing from us.

I moved them to the first time you deliberately step into your own space. Same two questions. Completely different relationship.

PHASE 0612–21 AUGUST · BOTH

Making true the things
that were pretending.

In August I audited my own codebase and wrote down everything that looked finished but wasn't. Then I spent the last stretch closing it.

Community was fabricated

An in-memory array of invented parents and invented expert replies, shipped to production and wiped on every reload. Now five real Postgres tables, with report, block and hide built in.

The Copilot didn't answer

It accepted a question and replied that it wasn't connected. It now runs a real model through a server-side function, scoped to your own child, with the conversation saved.

Same phase: the activity library went from 56 to 1,149, and "Milestones" became "Discoveries" — a milestone is something you can miss, a discovery is something that happens.

What the six phases taught me

Three things I'd
carry to the next build.

  • Prototype in the cheapest medium that can still be wrong. A webpage answered the riskiest question about this product before any app code existed. — Phase 01
  • If users have to learn your idea, delete the idea. The app briefly had two modes swapped by a toggle. It worked, but "which mode am I in" became something the user had to hold in their head. Five flat tabs replaced it and cost nothing. The toggle was a clever solution to a problem I had invented. — Phase 05
  • Audit your own work before someone else does. Writing down what was pretending to be finished was uncomfortable, and it directly produced the last two weeks of real work. — Phase 06
Being straight about it

What's real,
and what isn't.

Working

Onboarding, the four-activity Home, Discoveries, Vaccinations, the whole parent side, the Copilot, Community, and the entire data layer. Real accounts, real database, verified end to end against production.

Scaffolded

Reports, the Development Kit and the Product Guide are routed and designed, with content still to come. Courses and Workshops have structure and seeded lessons, but placeholder video URLs.

Needs a clinician

The vaccination schedule is transcribed accurately from the UIP and IAP sources, but it's medical content. It needs a paediatrician to sign it off before anyone relies on it — I'm not qualified to be the last set of eyes on that.

Not yet built

No analytics, which means I cannot currently answer "did anyone come back on day two?". No payments. And the waitlist has 11 signups — it isn't a pipeline yet, it's a form on a page nobody visits.
